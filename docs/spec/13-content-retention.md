## Part XIII: Content Retention Conflicts

### Chapter 39: When Rules Collide

**The content-addressing tension with right to erasure.** In a traditional relational database, deletion is a single SQL statement. The row is gone; the content is gone; the user's GDPR Art. 17 erasure right is satisfied. Content-addressed storage is categorically different. In IPFS, the CID is a deterministic hash of the content — the address is derived from the content and cannot be separated from it. Once an object is published to IPFS and any other node has pinned it, the original publisher cannot reach into that node and delete it. There is no IPFS API call for "please unpin this object from every node that has it."

Corundum's federated model makes this worse. When Bob's operator fetches Alice's post to display it to Bob (because Bob follows Alice), Bob's operator may pin the post to its own IPFS node for performance. That pin is entirely outside Alice's operator's control. When Alice deletes the post, Alice's operator unpins it from its own node. But Bob's operator's pin remains. From a content-addressing perspective, the content still exists on the network.

This is not unique to Corundum — it is a fundamental property of any content-addressed federated system. Corundum does not claim to solve it. What Corundum provides is the mechanisms that a GDPR-compliant deployment needs; compliance depends on operator behavior, contractual obligations between operators, and the legal framework applicable to the deployment.

**What "hidden but retained" means at the infrastructure level.** The archive tree never changes once written, so deletion does not modify the original entry. When a user deletes a post, the author's `Delete` activity (the post's tombstone; Chapter 13) is appended to the timeline, and the operator unpins the activity's bytes (and its SigningRecord) from its own node, and stops serving them. From the perspective of any client querying that operator, the content is gone. The tombstone is the authoritative record that the entry existed and was deleted. The original entry, including the deleted activity's identifier, remains in the tree (see "The identifier retained after deletion" below).

But other operators that previously pinned the content retain their pins until they are notified of the tombstone and comply with the erasure norm. The Corundum protocol cannot compel other IPFS nodes to unpin content — it can create obligations and mechanisms, not physical enforcement.

**The tombstone notification mechanism.** When a user's operator publishes a tombstone, it distributes the tombstone to operators known to have requested that content. "Known to have requested" includes: operators that submitted replies to that post (tracked in the reply protocol), operators that sent follow-relationship requests for the author, and operators that have active event stream subscriptions for the author's timeline. Those operators are expected to unpin the content from their IPFS nodes upon receiving the tombstone. This is a SHOULD-level obligation in the spec: operators SHOULD unpin content when notified of a tombstone by the authoritative operator. Operators that systematically ignore tombstone notifications violate the federation norms and risk having their ability to federate with compliant operators revoked.

**The conflict matrix.** Seven combinations of rules arise in practice:

- **User deletion request + no legal hold**: Append the tombstone entry, unpin the content from the operator's IPFS node, and distribute the tombstone to known downstream operators. Downstream operators SHOULD unpin on receipt. Distributing the tombstone is how the authoritative operator meets GDPR Art. 17(2), which requires a controller that made data public to take reasonable steps to inform other controllers processing it. Each downstream operator that pinned the content is itself a controller with its own Art. 17 obligations; residual pins are that operator's compliance problem, not the authoritative operator's.

- **User deletion request + legal hold**: Append the tombstone entry and stop serving the content to any client, but retain the content in the operator's own storage until the hold ends. A legal hold binds the party it is addressed to, not peer operators: the tombstone is distributed as usual, and downstream operators unpin as usual. This is not a GDPR violation where Art. 17(3)(b) (retention required by Union or Member State law) or Art. 17(3)(e) (establishment, exercise, or defence of legal claims) applies. The operator must document the legal basis and the end date of the hold in its own records.

- **Content expiration + no legal hold**: The activity's `expiresAt` has passed. `expiresAt` is part of the signed activity and is the only place expiry is recorded; because the archive tree never changes, no entry or retention metadata carries a second copy. Hide from display; the operator may unpin if it chooses (it is not required to retain expired content). This is not a GDPR erasure event — the content merely expired per the author's own setting. No tombstone notification obligation applies, though operators may choose to notify as a courtesy.

- **Curation mandate to hide**: A curator has flagged the content and the operator is applying the judgment by hiding it from display. The operator retains the content in storage for curation audit purposes (to be able to document what was hidden and why). This is not a GDPR erasure event, but the retained copy is still personal data: the operator needs a documented legal basis and a retention limit for it, and the user's data rights continue to apply.

- **Curation mandate + legal hold**: A curator has flagged content that is also under a legal hold. Same as user deletion + legal hold: hide from display and retain in the holder's own storage until the hold ends. Retention is compelled by the hold and binds only its holder. The curator flag is documented in the audit record.

- **Operator maximum retention exceeded + no legal hold**: The operator's retention policy (e.g., retain posts for 7 years) has expired and no legal hold or other obligation requires retention. The operator may delete its own copy. This is an operational decision by the operator; no GDPR obligation requires the operator to retain data indefinitely, and Art. 5(1)(e) (storage limitation) actually pushes in the direction of not retaining data longer than necessary.

- **User data export request (GDPR Art. 20, portability)**: The operator must export all non-tombstoned user-generated content. Tombstoned content is not required in the export — the user already deleted it and the tombstone records that deletion. The IPFS-based archive makes this technically straightforward: the ArchiveIndex enumerates all the user's entries, the operator skips entries named by a later `deletes:` tombstone and exports the latest version of entries named by `replaces:`, and the export is a ZIP or IPFS CAR file of those objects.

**The identifier retained after deletion.** Because the archive tree never changes, the original entry still holds the deleted activity's identifier: a raw-codec CID, which is the SHA-256 of the activity's stored bytes (Chapter 33). This identifier is personal data. A hash of personal data is pseudonymous, not anonymous: GDPR Recital 26 treats pseudonymised data as information on an identifiable person, and the Article 29 Working Party (Opinion 05/2014 on Anonymisation Techniques) classes hashing as pseudonymisation, not anonymisation. The identifier is also a retrieval key: anyone still holding a copy can find and serve it by that CID. And a short post can be confirmed by guessing: hash a candidate text and compare it to the identifier.

Corundum retains the identifier anyway, on a stated basis: the integrity of the archive. Removing it would change the CID of the epoch and of every structure above it, invalidating every copy and reference peers already hold, and keeping it prevents an operator from substituting different content at the same position and claiming the tombstone covers it. Operators relying on this rely on legitimate interest (GDPR Art. 6(1)(f)) and must document that assessment. What deletion removes: the activity's bytes and its SigningRecord from the operator's storage, and their service to clients. What it does not remove: the original entry, the activity identifier and other entry metadata in the immutable tree, and the tombstone entry itself.

**Corundum's explicit architectural boundary.** Corundum does not claim to make IPFS GDPR-proof. The protocol spec is not a data protection authority, cannot bind third-party IPFS nodes, and cannot guarantee that every federated operator complies with erasure requests. What Corundum provides is: a tombstone mechanism so deletion intent is unambiguously communicated, a notification mechanism so downstream operators can act on that intent, and conflict resolution rules so operators know what to do when obligations collide. A GDPR-compliant deployment of Corundum requires operators, which are independent controllers rather than each other's processors, to enter into data sharing agreements with each other that establish binding erasure obligations, and to implement the tombstone notification pathway faithfully. The protocol provides the plumbing; the compliance depends on the humans operating it.
